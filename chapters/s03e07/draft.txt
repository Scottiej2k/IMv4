# The informal *tu* imperative: telling someone what to do

## What it is

The imperative is the verb form you use to give an instruction, an order, an invitation or a piece of
advice: *Take the bowl. Stir! Cut the pasta. Turn on the oven.* In Italian, when you speak to **one
person you know well** — a family member, a friend, a child, a classmate — you use the **informal
*tú* imperative**. This chapter lives in the kitchen, and the kitchen is where the *tú* imperative
lives too: recipes are nothing but a long list of friendly orders.

## How it works

For regular verbs, the *tú* imperative is short and easy. It looks like the normal present-tense
form for *tú*, except for **-are** verbs, which end in **-a** instead of **-i**.

| Verb type | Present (*tu*) | Imperative (*tu*) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **-are** (parlare) | parli | **parla!** | speak! |
| **-are** (tagliare) | tagli | **taglia!** | cut! |
| **-are** (mescolare) | mescoli | **mescola!** | stir! |
| **-are** (versare) | versi | **versa!** | pour! |
| **-ere** (prendere) | prendi | **prendi!** | take! |
| **-ere** (mettere) | metti | **metti!** | put! |
| **-ere** (accendere) | accendi | **accendi!** | turn on! |
| **-ere** (cuocere) | cuoci | **cuoci!** | cook! |
| **-ire** (aprire) | apri | **apri!** | open! |
| **-ire** (dormire) | dormi | **dormi!** | sleep! |

So: **-ere** and **-ire** verbs are identical to the present tense; only **-are** verbs change
(*parli* → **parla**). There is no subject pronoun — you simply say **Prendi la ciotola**, not *tu
prendi la ciotola* (that would sound like a statement, not an order).

A few very common verbs are **irregular** in the *tú* imperative:

| Verb | Imperative | Example |
|---|---|---|
| andare | **va'** (or *vai*) | Va' a prendere il pane! |
| fare | **fa'** (or *fai*) | Fa' piano! |
| dare | **da'** (or *dai*) | Da' un pizzico di sale. |
| dire | **di'** | Di' la verità. |
| stare | **sta'** (or *stai*) | Sta' attento! |
| essere | **sii** | Sii gentile. |
| avere | **abbi** | Abbi pazienza. |

The short forms (*va'*, *fa'*, *da'*, *di'*, *sta'*) are the most elegant and the most common in
writing. When you add a pronoun, the short form doubles its consonant and joins it: *fa'* + *lo* →
**fallo**, *da'* + *mi* → **dammi**, *di'* + *lo* → **dillo**. You will study that pattern later in
the course, so for now just recognize it.

## Recipes: the natural home of the imperative

Italian recipe books are written almost entirely in the *tú* imperative, and Anna's notebook in this
chapter is no exception. Each line of a recipe is one little order after another: **taglia**, **metti**,
**aggiungi**, **versa**, **copri**, **cuoci**, **spegni**. When you read a recipe out loud to someone
else, you are giving those orders yourself — which is exactly what happens between Chiara and Lucia.

## From the story

- [[Metti gli spinaci sul tavolo.]] — *mettere* in the *tu* form; Franco tells Chiara what to do with the shopping, like a small command at home.
- [[Prendi la ciotola.]] — Kevin turns cooking into a sequence of instructions: *prendere* is regular, so the imperative looks like the present.
- [[Adesso aggiungi la farina.]] — *aggiungere* keeps its *-gi-* sound before the final *-i*.
- [[Adesso versa il latte.]] — a classic *-are* verb: the ending becomes **-a**, not **-i**.
- [[Mescola! No, piano!]] — imperative plus adverb: Leo stirs too fast, and Kevin corrects him with one word.
- [[Taglia la pasta in strisce larghe.]] — *tagliare* is *-are*, so the *tu* imperative is **taglia**; here it comes straight from the notebook.
- [[Accendi il fuoco.]] — *accendere* is *-ere*: the imperative is identical to *tu accendi* in the present tense.
- [[Cuoci per quaranta minuti.]] — *cuocere* is irregular in shape but follows the *-ere* rule for the imperative: **cuoci**.
- [[Taglia un'altra fetta, Matteo.]] — an imperative often carries a name at the end, which softens the order into a request.

## Common mistakes English speakers make

1. **Using the infinitive.** English recipes say *cut, add, cook* (no subject), so learners often say
   *tagliare la pasta*. That is not an instruction — it means "to cut the pasta." Say **Taglia la
   pasta**.
2. **Keeping the *-are* verb in *-i*.** *Parli piano!* sounds like a statement ("you speak slowly"),
   not an order. For *-are* verbs the imperative is **-a**: **Parla piano!**
3. **Adding the pronoun *tu*.** *Tu prendi la ciotola* is a description, not a command. Leave *tu* out.
4. **Using it with strangers.** The *tu* imperative is for family, friends and children. With a
   shopkeeper, an official or someone you have just met, Italian uses the formal *Lei* form, which you
   will learn later.
5. **Forgetting that the negative is different.** *Non parlare!* is not the same pattern, and it is
   taught in a later chapter — for now, use the *tu* imperative only in the affirmative.
