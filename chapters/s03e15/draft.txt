# Diminutives and Augmentatives: *-ino*, *-one*, *-accio*

## What they are

Italian loves to change the size and the feeling of a word by adding a little ending to it. You take a word, cut off its final vowel, and glue on a special suffix:

- **naso** → **nasino** ("little nose")
- **caffè** → **caffettino** ("a nice little coffee")
- **ragazzo** → **ragazzone** ("a big guy")

These changed words are called *alterati*. They are not separate vocabulary to memorize one by one — they are the same word wearing a costume. That's why in this episode Lucia can say **nasino**, **manina**, **piedino**, **bacino**, **lettino**, **tutina**, **golfino**, **storiella**, and everyone understands her immediately.

The suffix does two things at once: it tells you the **size**, and it tells you the **feeling** of the speaker.

## How it works

| Suffix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **-ino / -ina** | small, and usually affectionate | nasino, manina, lettino, piedino, momentino, caffettino |
| **-etto / -etta** | small, often neat or dainty | caffettino → caffè; libretto, cassette, casetta |
| **-ello / -ella** | small, literary or fixed | alberello, ruscello, storiella |
| **-one / -ona** | big (and often impressive or funny) | ragazzone, cappuccinone, donnone, librone |
| **-accio / -accia** | bad, unpleasant, of poor quality | parolaccia, tempaccio, ragazzaccio |

**Gender and number follow the original word.** *naso* is masculine, so *nasino* is masculine (*il nasino*, *i nasini*). *mano* is feminine even though it ends in *-o*, so *manina* is feminine: *la manina*, *le manine*. A common pitfall with the body is *dito* → *il ditino*, *i ditini*.

**A few of these words have become ordinary vocabulary.** Italians no longer hear a "small" idea inside *telefonino* (mobile phone), *fratellino*, or *giornalino*. They are just the normal words. This is why the textbook teaches *il fratellino* as vocabulary rather than as a suffix exercise.

**Two suffixes can stack:** *cappuccio* → *cappuccino* → *cappuccinone*. English did the same thing when it borrowed *cappuccino*.

**When not to use them.** Diminutives are affectionate, so they can sound babyish or ironic with adults. Lucia can call a cup of coffee a *caffettino*; a businessman in a meeting should not. With a stranger, keep the plain word.

## From the story

[[Ma guarda questo nasino piccolino!]]
Lucia takes the plain word *naso* and makes it tiny and loving in the same breath.

[[E le manine... che carine!]]
*mano* is feminine, so the suffix is *-ine*, and the adjective agrees: *carine*.

[[Guarda che piedini!]]
The suffix carries all the emotion: no adjective is needed to say "how sweet."

[[Tieni la bambina un momentino.]]
A *momentino* is not really about time — it's a polite, soft way of asking.

[[Allora faccio un caffettino: metà caffè, metà latte, un po' di zucchero.]]
Matteo hopes the suffix will sell his invention. It doesn't.

[[Questo è una parolaccia in tazza.]]
Here *-accia* means "bad, ugly, unpleasant": a word you shouldn't say.

[[Il cappuccinone è carino.]]
The same suffix *-one* that makes *ragazzone* makes an enormous cappuccino.

[[Un bacino non è un bacio piccolo. È un bacio importante.]]
Kevin's insight: in Italian, the diminutive often makes a thing *bigger* in feeling, not smaller.

[[Le mie ditini sono piccoli.]] / [[I ditini, non le ditini.]]
Leo uses the wrong gender; Ornella corrects him in the same scene. *ditino* is masculine.

## Common mistakes English speakers make

1. **Treating every *-ino* as "small."** *Un caffettino* is not necessarily a tiny coffee; it's a warm, friendly one. *Un attimino* is just "just a second."
2. **Getting the gender wrong with the body.** *mano* is feminine (*la manina*), but *dito* is masculine (*il ditino*). Don't guess from the *-o* or *-a* ending.
3. **Forgetting agreement.** The adjective must match the altered noun: *le manine sono carine*, *i piedini sono freddi*.
4. **Using *-one* to mean "very."** *Ragazzone* is a big guy, but *un problemone* is a serious problem — the suffix adds size, weight or humor, not just intensity.
5. **Using diminutives with strangers.** With a shopkeeper or an official you say *un caffè*, not *un caffettino*. Save the sugar for family and friends.
6. **Double diminutives.** Don't stack two affectionate suffixes (*nasinino*). One is enough.
