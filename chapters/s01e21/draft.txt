# Partitives and quantities: "some," "a kilo of," and prices

## What it is

English uses words like *some* and *any* very freely: *some bread, some apples, some cheese*. Italian does the same thing with a set of little words called **partitives**: **del, dello, della, dei, degli, delle**. They mean "some" or "a bit of" and they appear in front of a noun you can't count exactly — food, money, shopping, time.

The second half of this lesson is about **quantities**: how Italians say *a kilo of tomatoes*, *a hundred grams of cheese*, *a bottle of water*, and how prices work. In Italian, a quantity is almost always followed by **di** (or one of its forms): *un chilo **di** pane*, *un litro **di** latte*.

Finally, you'll meet **numbers above 100** and the phrase for asking prices: **quanto costa?** / **quanto costano?**

## How it works

### 1. The partitive: "some"

The partitive is just **di + article**, joined into one word:

| | singular | plural |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | **del** pane, **dello** zucchero, **dell'** olio | **dei** pomodori, **degli** etti |
| feminine | **della** carne, **dell'** acqua | **delle** mele, **delle** bottiglie |

Use the partitive when you mean *an indefinite amount*:

> Vorrei **del** pane, **delle** mele e **dell'** acqua.

You can often replace it with **un po' di** ("a bit of", "some"), which never changes:

> **un po' di** formaggio — **un po' di** frutta — **un po' di** sconto

**Un po' di** is always followed by a singular noun, even for things you'd expect plural in English.

### 2. Quantities: quantity + **di**

A quantity phrase is **number/amount + di + noun**. The word after **di** names *what* you're counting.

| Italian | English |
|---|---|
| **un chilo di** pomodori | a kilo of tomatoes |
| **un etto di** formaggio | a hundred grams of cheese |
| **mezzo chilo di** mele | half a kilo of apples |
| **un litro di** latte | a liter of milk |
| **una bottiglia di** acqua | a bottle of water |
| **sei chili di** pane | six kilos of bread |

**Un etto** is the word you need at every Italian market: it's **100 grammi**. So *un etto di carne* is 100 g of meat, and *due etti* is 200 g. The **bilancia** at the **banco** does the weighing for you.

Note: with **cento grammi di**, **mezzo chilo di**, etc., the noun after **di** stays singular: *mezzo chilo **di** mela* (as a quantity of apple). But Italian often uses the plural when you buy individual items: *mezzo chilo **di** mele*.

### 3. Numbers above 100

From 100 up, Italian numbers are easy: write the parts and read them one after another.

- **100** cento — **101** centouno — **110** centodieci
- **200** duecento — **300** trecento — **400** quattrocento
- **360** trecentosessanta — **500** cinquecento — **1000** mille

You don't say *"one hundred"* — you just say **cento**. And **mille** stays singular even after a big number in front of it. Hundreds agree in gender: **duecento euro** (euro doesn't change in the plural), **duecento mele**.

### 4. Prices and "quanto costa?"

The key question is:

> **Quanto costa?** (one thing) → **Quanto costano?** (more things)

The answer uses **al chilo** ("per kilo"), **all'etto**, **al litro**:

> Le mele, **due euro al chilo**. La carne, **dodici euro al chilo**.

At the checkout you'll hear **il resto** ("the change") and you can ask for **lo sconto** ("the discount").

## Common mistakes English speakers make

- **Forgetting **di** after a quantity.** Say *un chilo **di** pane*, not *un chilo pane*. This is the single most common slip.
- **Using **molto** instead of **un po' di**.** *Un po' di* means "a small amount"; *molto* means "a lot". Different jobs.
- **Making **un po' di** plural.** It's always followed by a singular noun: *un po' di **mela***, not *un po' di mele*.
- **Mixing up **del** and **di**.** *Di* alone is a simple preposition: *bottiglia di acqua* (no article). *Del* is a partitive: *Vorrei **del** vino*. Choose by meaning.
- **Word-for-word numbers.** Don't say *"un cento"*. It's just **cento**. And **mille**, not *"un mille"*.
- **Quanto costa / quanto costano.** Match the verb to the thing you're asking about.
