# Stare per + infinitive: "to be about to"

## What it is

*Stare per* + infinitive is how Italian says that something is **about to happen** — in a moment, right now, very soon. In English we often use "about to" or "just going to":

- *Sto per uscire.* = I'm about to go out.
- *Sta per piovere.* = It's about to rain.

The whole expression is *stare per* + the **infinitive** of the main verb. Nothing else goes between *per* and the infinitive — no subject, no adverb.

## How it works

You conjugate only **stare**; the other verb stays in the infinitive, every time.

| Subject | stare | + per | + infinitive | English |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| io | **sto** | per | partire | I'm about to leave |
| tu | **stai** | per | partire | you're about to leave |
| lui / lei | **sta** | per | partire | he/she is about to leave |
| noi | **stiamo** | per | partire | we're about to leave |
| voi | **state** | per | partire | you're about to leave |
| loro | **stanno** | per | partire | they're about to leave |

The infinitive never changes: *sto per nascere* is wrong from a person, but *sta per nascere* works for the baby, and *stanno per nascere* for twins. Learn the shape **stare + per + infinitive** as one block.

To make it negative, put *non* before *stare*:

- *Non sto per stare qui.* = I'm not about to stay here.

Yes — *stare* can appear twice: once as the helper, once as the real verb.

It works in the **imperfetto** too, for something that was about to happen in the past:

- *Stavo per uscire quando ha chiamato Matteo.* = I was about to go out when Matteo called.
- *Stavamo per partire.* = We were about to leave.

## Stare per vs. stare + gerund

These two look similar but mean different things.

- **stare per + infinitive** = about to do something (it hasn't started yet).
  *Sta per nascere.* = The baby is about to be born.
- **stare + gerund (-ando / -endo)** = doing something right now, in progress.
  *Sta nascendo.* = The baby is being born right now.

In this chapter everyone is stuck in the moment *before*: nobody is doing anything yet, they are all *about to*. That is why *stare per* appears again and again — it is the tense of waiting.

## From the story

«E io **sto per** chiudere» dice Matteo. «È la vigilia.» — “And I'm **about to** close,” says Matteo. “It's Christmas Eve.” (`s03e13-1-010`) — the same structure, opposite verb: Matteo is about to close it.
«Non **sto per** stare qui» dice Nadia. «Andiamo!» — “I'm not **about to** stay here,” says Nadia. “Let's go!” (`s03e13-1-072`) — negative form, and *stare* used both as helper and as the main verb.
«**Sta per** **nascere**» dice Nadia, piano. — “She's **about to** **be born**,” says Nadia, quietly. (`s03e13-2-090`) — third person singular, the sentence that gives the episode its title.
«C'è troppo **ghiaccio** davanti» dice Kevin. «**Sto per** aprire lo **sportello**.» — “There's too much **ice** in front,” says Kevin. “I'm **about to** open the **door**.” (`s03e13-3-041`) — the infinitive carries its own object without any change to *sto per*.
«Lo **sto per** dire da venti minuti» dice Matteo. — “I've been **about to** say that for twenty minutes,” says Matteo. (`s03e13-4-009`) — a pronoun can sit before *stare*, but never between *per* and the infinitive.
«Un momento» dice l'uomo. «**Sto per** finire.» — “One moment,” says the man. “I'm **about to** finish.” (`s03e13-4-008`) — a short, complete sentence: no object is needed for the meaning to work.

Notice how many of these are in the **present**. Everyone keeps announcing what is about to happen; nobody has done it yet.

## Common mistakes English speakers make

- **Adding a word after *per*.** English says "about to **go**", but Italian keeps only the infinitive: *sto per andare*, never *sto per a andare* or *sto per di andare*.
- **Conjugating the second verb.** Learners write *sto per vado* or *sta per nasce*. Only *stare* changes; the other verb stays an infinitive.
- **Confusing *per* with *di* or *a*.** Many Italian verbs need *di* or *a* before an infinitive (*provo a*, *decido di*). *Stare* needs **per**. Memorize the three together.
- **Using the future instead.** "The baby will be born soon" tempts learners into the *futuro*. But when it is happening in seconds, Italian prefers *sta per nascere*, not a future form.
- **Forgetting the negative position.** *Non* goes before *stare*: *non sta per arrivare*, not *sta non per arrivare*.
