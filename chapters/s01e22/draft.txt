# Sapere vs conoscere: two ways to say "to know"

English has one verb, *to know*, and uses it for everything: *I know your sister*, *I know how to swim*, *I know her phone number*. Italian splits that meaning in two, and the two verbs are almost never interchangeable. Choosing the wrong one is one of the most common mistakes an English speaker makes, and it is also very easy to fix.

## What they are

**Sapere** = to know a *fact*, a piece of information, or *how to do* something.
**Conoscere** = to know a *person*, a *place*, or something you are *familiar with* (a song, a book, a city, a shop).

So: *conosco* Lucia (I know Lucia, the person) but *so* che Lucia balla (I know that Lucia dances, the fact). *Conosco* Borgoverde (I know the town) but *so* dov'è il bar (I know where the bar is).

## How they work

Both are regular in the present tense except that **sapere** is irregular in the *io* form and **conoscere** adds **-sc-** in four forms:

| | **sapere** | **conoscere** |
|---|---|---|
| io | **so** | **conosco** |
| tu | **sai** | **conosci** |
| lui / lei | **sa** | **conosce** |
| noi | **sappiamo** | **conosciamo** |
| voi | **sapete** | **conoscete** |
| loro | **sanno** | **conoscono** |

**Sapere** is followed by:
- a fact or a thing: *so il nome*, *so la strada*, *non so niente*
- **che** + a sentence: *so che balla*
- an infinitive (a skill): *so nuotare*, *so fare i pancake*
- a question word: *non so chi canta*, *non so dove è*

**Conoscere** is followed by a noun: a person (*conosco Matteo*), a place (*conosci il centro civico?*), or something you know by experience (*conosco questa canzone*).

## From the story

[[Sai usare la macchina?]] — *sapere* + infinitive: knowing **how to** do something.
[[Tu sai fare i pancake?]] — same pattern, with a skill that matters a lot in this episode.
[[So nuotare. So cucinare. So una cosa.]] — the same verb, first with abilities and then with a plain fact.
[[Sai nuotare?]] — a short, everyday question about an ability: *Sai* + infinitive.
[[Ma sai insegnare?]] — a skill again, and the question that changes the evening for Chiara.
[[Allora io conosco Lucia e so che balla]] — the two verbs in one sentence: *conoscere* a person, *sapere* a fact about her.
[[Conosco Lucia, conosco Matteo, conosco Nadia.]] — *conoscere* only, repeated with people.
[[Non conosco Anna]] — *conoscere* negated: Lucia has never met Anna. The next sentence, [[Ma so una cosa di lei.]], uses *sapere* for one fact about her.
[[Lucia sa una cosa di mia madre]] — *sapere* + a fact: the sentence that starts Chiara's evening at home.
[[Adesso io so quasi tutto della macchina.]] — *sapere* + *tutto*: information, not a person.

## Common mistakes

- **"So Lucia"** for *I know Lucia*. If it is a person, you need **conosco Lucia**. Matteo, Nadia and everyone else in Borgoverde will notice.
- **"Conosco nuotare"** for *I know how to swim*. Abilities always take **sapere**: *so nuotare*, *so guidare*, *so cantare*.
- **"Conosco che…"** does not exist. Facts and clauses start with **so che…**: *so che Lucia canta bene*.
- **Places take *conoscere*** when you mean "to be familiar with": *conosci Milano?* — *Do you know Milan?* Use *sapere* only if you are talking about information: *sai dov'è Milano?*
- **"Non so Lucia"** vs **"Non conosco Lucia"**: the first means "I don't know that fact", the second means "I have never met her".
- Watch the *io* form: it is **so**, one syllable, not *sapo*.
