# Sapere vs conoscere: two ways to say "to know"

English has one verb, *to know*, and uses it for everything: *I know your sister*, *I know how to swim*, *I know her phone number*. Italian splits that meaning in two, and the two verbs are almost never interchangeable. Choosing the wrong one is one of the most common mistakes an English speaker makes, and it is also very easy to fix.

## What they are

**Sapere** = to know a *fact*, a piece of information, or *how to do* something.
**Conoscere** = to know a *person*, a *place*, or something you are *familiar with* (a song, a book, a city, a shop).

So: *conosco* Lucia (I know Lucia, the person) but *so* che Lucia balla (I know that Lucia dances, the fact). *Conosco* Borgoverde (I know the town) but *so* dov'è il bar (I know where the bar is).

## How they work

Both are regular in the present tense except that **sapere** is irregular in the *io* form and **conoscere** adds **-sc-** in four forms:

| | **sapere** | **conoscere** |
|---|---|---|
| io | **so** | **conosco** |
| tu | **sai** | **conosci** |
| lui / lei | **sa** | **conosce** |
| noi | **sappiamo** | **conosciamo** |
| voi | **sapete** | **conoscete** |
| loro | **sanno** | **conoscono** |

**Sapere** is followed by:
- a fact or a thing: *so il nome*, *so la strada*, *non so niente*
- **che** + a sentence: *so che balla*
- an infinitive (a skill): *so nuotare*, *so fare i pancake*
- a question word: *non so chi canta*, *non so dove è*

**Conoscere** is followed by a noun: a person (*conosco Matteo*), a place (*conosci il centro civico?*), or something you know by experience (*conosco questa canzone*).

## From the story

«Perfetto!» risponde Kevin. «**Sai** usare la **macchina**?» — “Perfect!” Kevin answers. “**Do you know** how to use the **machine**?” (`s01e22-1-009`) — *sapere* + infinitive: knowing **how to** do something.
«Tu **sai** fare i pancake?» chiede Matteo. — “You **know** how to make pancakes?” Matteo asks. (`s01e22-1-019`) — same pattern, with a skill that matters a lot in this episode.
«**So** nuotare. **So** cucinare. **So** una cosa.» — “I **know** how to swim. I **know** how to cook. I **know** a thing.” (`s01e22-1-056`) — the same verb, first with abilities and then with a plain fact.
«**Sai** **nuotare**?» chiede Lucia. — “**Do you know** how to **swim**?” Lucia asks. (`s01e22-4-028`) — a short, everyday question about an ability: *Sai* + infinitive.
«Ma **sai** **insegnare**?» chiede Lucia. — “But **do you know** how to **teach**?” Lucia asks. (`s01e22-4-042`) — a skill again, and the question that changes the evening for Chiara.
«Allora io **conosco** Lucia e **so** che balla» dice. — “So I **know** Lucia and I **know** that she dances,” he says. (`s01e22-1-058`) — the two verbs in one sentence: *conoscere* a person, *sapere* a fact about her.
«**Conosco** Lucia, **conosco** Matteo, **conosco** Nadia.» — “I **know** Lucia, I **know** Matteo, I **know** Nadia.” (`s01e22-1-054`) — *conoscere* only, repeated with people.
«Non **conosco** Anna» dice Lucia. «Ma **so** una cosa di lei.» — “I don't **know** Anna,” Lucia says. “But I **know** one thing about her.” (`s01e22-5-019`) — *conoscere* negated: Lucia has never met Anna. The next sentence, «Non **conosco** Anna» dice Lucia. «Ma **so** una cosa di lei.» — “I don't **know** Anna,” Lucia says. “But I **know** one thing about her.” (`s01e22-5-019`), uses *sapere* for one fact about her.
«Lucia **sa** una cosa di mia madre» dice. — “Lucia **knows** one thing about my mother,” she says. (`s01e22-6-015`) — *sapere* + a fact: the sentence that starts Chiara's evening at home.
«Sì» dice Matteo. «Adesso io **so** quasi **tutto** della **macchina**.» — “Yes,” Matteo says. “Now I **know** almost **everything** about the **machine**.” (`s01e22-7-008`) — *sapere* + *tutto*: information, not a person.

## Common mistakes

- **"So Lucia"** for *I know Lucia*. If it is a person, you need **conosco Lucia**. Matteo, Nadia and everyone else in Borgoverde will notice.
- **"Conosco nuotare"** for *I know how to swim*. Abilities always take **sapere**: *so nuotare*, *so guidare*, *so cantare*.
- **"Conosco che…"** does not exist. Facts and clauses start with **so che…**: *so che Lucia canta bene*.
- **Places take *conoscere*** when you mean "to be familiar with": *conosci Milano?* — *Do you know Milan?* Use *sapere* only if you are talking about information: *sai dov'è Milano?*
- **"Non so Lucia"** vs **"Non conosco Lucia"**: the first means "I don't know that fact", the second means "I have never met her".
- Watch the *io* form: it is **so**, one syllable, not *sapo*.

### Answer key

1. **conosco** (person)
2. **sai** (sapere + infinitive)
3. **sappiamo** (sapere + question word)
4. **Conosci** (person)
5. **conosce** (person — everyone, except the mayor)
6. **so** … **so** (ability, then fact with *che*)
7. **sapete** (fact with *che*)
8. **conosce** (person you are familiar with)
