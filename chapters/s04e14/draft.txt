# Impersonal *si* with reflexive verbs; *si* + adjectives

## What it is

In Italian you often make a sentence about "people in general" instead of one specific person. You do this by putting **si** in front of a third-person-singular verb:

- *In Italia **si beve** molto caffè.* — In Italy, people drink a lot of coffee.
- ***Si lavora** fino a tardi.* — People work late. / You work late.

This is called the **impersonal *si***. There is no real subject: nobody in particular is doing the action. English often solves the same problem with "you", "one", "people", or "they".

The tricky part comes when the verb is **reflexive** (*alzarsi*, *abituarsi*, *arrabbiarsi*…) or when it is followed by an **adjective that agrees with people**, not with a thing. That is what this lesson is about.

## How it works

### 1. Plain impersonal *si*

Take the *lui/lei* form (third person singular) and put **si** in front:

| Verb | Impersonal *si* |
|---|---|
| mangiare | **si mangia** |
| lavorare | **si lavora** |
| entrare | **si entra** |

### 2. Impersonal *si* with a reflexive verb → **ci si**

If the verb is reflexive, we would need two *si* in a row: *~~si si alza~~*. Italian avoids that double *si* by changing the first one to **ci**:

| Reflexive verb | Impersonal form |
|---|---|
| alzarsi | **ci si alza** |
| abituarsi | **ci si abitua** |
| arrabbiarsi | **ci si arrabbia** |
| sentirsi | **ci si sente** |
| ambientarsi | **ci si ambienta** |

So the rule is simple: **reflexive verb + impersonal meaning → ci si + third-person-singular verb.** The verb itself stays singular, exactly as with a normal impersonal *si*.

### 3. *si* + adjectives: plural adjectives

When an impersonal *si* sentence describes people with an adjective, the adjective is **plural** (usually masculine plural, because it includes men and women):

- ***Si è** sempre **stanchi** dopo il lavoro.* — You are always tired after work.
- *In montagna **si sta tranquilli**.* — In the mountains you are calm.
- *A tavola **si è** persone, non impiegati.* — At the table you are people, not employees.

Think of the adjective as referring to all those unnamed people: *si è* + adjective → adjective in *-i*.

Compare it with an adverb, which does not change: *si mangia **bene**, si sta **male***.

### 4. Impersonal *si* in compound tenses

With a compound tense (*passato prossimo* and the others), the auxiliary is *essere* and the past participle is **invariable** — it stays in the masculine singular *-o* form:

- ***Si è mangiato bene** ieri sera.* — People ate well last night.
- ***Si è partiti** presto.* — People left early.
- ***Ci si è abituati** poco a poco.* — People got used to it little by little.

Even if many people are involved, you never write *si è mangiati* or *si è partite*.

## From the story

[[Ci si alza, si va in corridoio, si mangia la merenda, e subito si torna in classe.]]
*Ci si alza* (reflexive, impersonal) next to plain *si va / si mangia / si torna* — the same sentence contrasts both patterns.

[[Ci si abitua a tutto. Al ritmo, al caos, alle tre pause caffè, al ponte di giugno.]]
*Ci si abitua* + a + a list of things: the impersonal form takes the preposition just like the personal verb.

[[E ci si abitua alle cose piccole: ai rumori, ai nomi, ai caffè. Alle cose grandi, invece, non ci si abitua mai.]]
The negative is made by putting *non* in front of *ci si abitua*: *non ci si abitua mai*.

[[All'inizio ci si sente stupidi. Tutto il giorno. Perché non capisci, perché sbagli, perché ti arrabbi con te stesso.]]
*Ci si sente* + plural adjective *stupidi*: the adjective refers to the unnamed people, not to a single person.

[[Alle cose grandi non ci si abitua. Prima o poi capisci che non devi diventare italiano. Devi soltanto stare bene qui.]]
*Ci si abitua* in a sentence about everybody who comes to a new country.

[[A tavola no. A tavola si mangia bene e si sta tranquilli.]]
*si mangia bene* (adverb, invariable) and *si sta tranquilli* (adjective, plural) in the same short sentence.

[[Perché la pausa pranzo non è una pausa dal lavoro. È il momento in cui si è persone.]]
*si è* + plural noun *persone*: with the impersonal *si*, even *essere* takes a plural noun.

[[Le persone che ami. Quelle non ci si abitua mai ad averle lontane.]]
The classic *ci si abitua* in a negative sentence, with *mai* after the verb.

[[Il mestiere si impara facendolo, non scrivendolo.]]
Plain impersonal *si* with a transitive verb: *si impara* means "you learn / people learn" — no specific learner in mind.

## Common mistakes

English speakers make the same handful of errors every time:

1. **Writing *si si*.** ~~*si si alza*~~ → **ci si alza**. Whenever a reflexive verb meets the impersonal *si*, the first *si* becomes **ci**.
2. **Making the verb plural.** ~~*ci si alzano*~~ → **ci si alza**. The impersonal *si* always keeps the verb in the third person **singular**, even when lots of people are involved.
3. **Forgetting to pluralize the adjective.** ~~*si è stanco*~~ → **si è stanchi** (or *stanche* if it is only about women). The adjective agrees with the imaginary crowd, not with one person.
4. **Agreeing the past participle with the crowd.** ~~*si è mangiati bene*~~ → **si è mangiato bene**. In impersonal *si* with a compound tense, the participle never changes.
5. **Confusing impersonal *si* with *si passivante*.** *Si vendono i biglietti* ("tickets are sold") has a real plural subject. *Si mangia bene* has no subject at all. This chapter is only about the second type.
6. **Using *si* with *tu* or *io*.** ~~*si sono abituato*~~ → **mi sono abituato**. If you mean yourself, use the personal reflexive — the impersonal *si* is for "people in general".
