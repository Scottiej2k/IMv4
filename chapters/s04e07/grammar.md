# Relative pronouns, part 2: *il quale*, *ciò che*, *quello che*, *chi*

## What it is

A **relative pronoun** joins a describing clause to a noun you already mentioned. English uses *who*, *which*, *that*: *the house **that** I like*, *the man **who** serves the tables*.

Italian has several ways to do this, and they are **not** interchangeable. This lesson is about four of them:

- **il quale / la quale / i quali / le quali** — *which / who*, the formal version of *che*;
- **ciò che** — *what*, *that which* — a thing, not a person;
- **quello che** — *what*, *the thing that* — same job, more common in speech;
- **chi** — *whoever*, *he who*, *the one who* — always people, always generic.

## How it works

### 1. *il quale* and its family

*Che* is the everyday relative pronoun, and you already know it: *la casa **che** mi piace*. **Il quale** says the same thing but sounds formal, written, or legal.

| singular | plural |
|---|---|
| il quale | i quali |
| la quale | le quali |

It agrees in gender and number with the noun it describes:

- *Il regolamento, **il quale** è del 1972, non si cambia.* — The rulebook, **which** is from 1972, doesn't get changed.
- *Le tovaglie, **le quali** sono di cotone, vanno lavate.* — The tablecloths, **which** are cotton, have to be washed.

Two practical points. **Il quale** is very useful after a preposition, because *che* cannot follow one: *il tavolo **sul quale** mangiamo* (the table **on which** we eat), *la scala **sulla quale** è salito Kevin*. And it is useful to avoid confusion when there are two possible nouns: *il padre di Lucia, **il quale** abita qui*.

### 2. *ciò che* and *quello che*

Both mean *what* or *the thing(s) that*. They are pronouns with no noun of their own: they stand alone.

- **ciò che** — more formal, more written, a little abstract.
- **quello che** — the everyday spoken form. It changes with the noun it replaces: *quello che*, *quella che*, *quelli che*, *quelle che*.

| Italian | English |
|---|---|
| **Ciò che** dico è vero. | **What** I say is true. |
| **Quello che** voglio è un posto semplice. | **What** I want is a simple place. |
| **Quelle che** hai visto sono le sedie. | **The ones** you saw are the chairs. |

With **tutto**: *tutto ciò che* and *tutto quello che* both mean *everything that*. *Tutto ciò che* is the more formal of the two.

- *Tutto **ciò che** non si può fare.* — Everything **that** you can't do.

### 3. *chi*

**Chi** as a relative pronoun means *whoever*, *anyone who*, *the one who*. It refers only to people, it is always singular in form, and it never needs an article.

- ***Chi** va piano va sano e va lontano.* — **He who** goes slowly goes safely and goes far.
- ***Chi** entra sa già dove mettersi.* — **Whoever** comes in already knows where to stand.
- *Non so **chi** ha ragione.* — I don't know **who** is right.

Notice the last one: there, *chi* is an indirect question ("who?"), not a relative pronoun. Both exist; context decides.

### 4. Which one to choose

| You mean | Use |
|---|---|
| the thing/person, with the noun still there | *che*, or formal ***il quale / la quale*** |
| the thing, with the noun gone | ***quello che*** (speech) / ***ciò che*** (formal) |
| everything, noun gone | ***tutto quello che*** / ***tutto ciò che*** |
| a person, generic, no noun | ***chi*** |

## From the story

«Ma nessuna di queste tre è **ciò che** vogliamo.» — “But none of these three is **what** we want.” (`s04e07-1-066`)
*Ciò che* stands alone as the subject: nothing is named, "the thing we want" stays open.

«È **ciò che** vogliamo.» — “It's **what** we want.” (`s04e07-6-045`)
The same phrase as a short, final answer — this is how *ciò che* works best: as a whole idea in place of a noun.

«Non è **quello che** vogliamo.» — “It's not **what** we want.” (`s04e07-3-074`)
*Quello che*, the spoken twin of *ciò che*. Franco uses both in the same afternoon, and both are correct.

«E **quello che** non capisce, me lo chieda.» «Lei è l'unico, in questa strada, che legge le regole prima di firmare.» — “And **what** you don't understand, ask me.” “You're the only one on this street who reads the rules before signing.” (`s04e07-4-100`)
*Quello che* as the object of the sentence, with the noun left out entirely.

«Ho scritto: tutto **ciò che** NON si può.» — “I wrote: everything **that** you CANNOT do.” (`s04e07-4-075`)
*Tutto* + *ciò che*: everything, without exception. Note the more formal register — Kevin copied it from Roberto's file.

«E **chi** serve a tavola?» — “And **who** serves at the table?” (`s04e07-1-023`)
*Chi* used twice in one morning: *who serves*, *who blows out the candles*. No noun, only people.

«Bravo. Senti che cosa dice il nonno: **chi** va piano va sano e va lontano.» — “Good boy. Listen to what your grandpa says: **he who** goes slowly goes safely and goes far.” (`s04e07-1-043`)
A proverb. *Chi* here is *he who*, *anyone who* — the classic use, and the one learners meet first.

«Vuol dire che **chi** ha fretta sbaglia strada.» «E tua madre ha molta fretta.» — “It means that **whoever** is in a hurry takes the wrong road.” “And your mother is in a big hurry.” (`s04e07-1-045`)
Franco turns the proverb into a general rule: *whoever is in a hurry takes the wrong road*.

«È bello, sì.» «Ma il posto giusto è un posto dove **chi** entra sa già dove mettersi.» — “It's beautiful, yes.” “But the right place is a place where **whoever** comes in already knows where to stand.” (`s04e07-3-076`)
*Chi* inside a *dove* clause: whoever walks in.

«L'ho visto.» «Mio padre ha scelto l'unico posto che non si può prenotare.» — “I saw it.” “My father chose the only place that can't be booked.” (`s04e07-6-079`)
With a named noun (*l'unico posto*), Italian uses plain *che*, not *il quale* — and certainly not *chi*.

«E **quello che** non capisce, me lo chieda.» «Lei è l'unico, in questa strada, che legge le regole prima di firmare.» — “And **what** you don't understand, ask me.” “You're the only one on this street who reads the rules before signing.” (`s04e07-4-100`)
A relative clause about a person, introduced by *che*, separated by commas for emphasis.

## Common mistakes

- **Using *chi* with a noun.** *Il posto **chi** conosco* is wrong. Use *che*: *il posto **che** conosco*. *Chi* replaces the whole noun, it doesn't follow one.
- **Translating *what* with *che*.** *Che voglio è un orto* is wrong; *what I want* is ***quello che** voglio* or ***ciò che** voglio*.
- **Using *il quale* where *che* is normal.** In conversation, *la casa **la quale** ho comprato* sounds like a contract. Say *la casa **che** ho comprato*. Keep *il quale* for writing, official language, and after prepositions.
- **Forgetting agreement with *il quale*.** It follows the noun, not the speaker: *le tovaglie, **le quali***.
- **Mixing up *ciò che* and *quello che* in tone.** Both are right, but *ciò che* in a chat message sounds stiff. In speech, *quello che*.
- **Adding *che* after *chi*.** *Chi che va piano* is wrong. *Chi* already contains the *who*.

Fill in with *chi*, *ciò che*, *quello che* (or *tutto ciò che*), or *il quale / la quale / i quali / le quali*.

5. Le sedie, ______ sono in magazzino, sono centocinquanta.
6. Ha fatto ______ poteva.

8. Il tavolo sul ______ mangiamo è nel cortile.

**Answer key**

1. *Chi* — whoever, people only, no noun.
2. *quello che* — *what you're saying*, noun gone (spoken).
3. *Quello che* (or *Ciò che*) — *what I like*, as a whole idea.
4. *il quale* — formal, after a comma, agreeing with *regolamento* (masculine singular).
5. *le quali* — agreeing with *sedie* (feminine plural).
6. *quello che* (or *ciò che*) — *he did what he could*.
7. *Chi* — the proverb form, *he who*.
8. *quale* — after a preposition (*sul*), which *che* cannot follow.
