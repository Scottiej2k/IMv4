# Subject pronouns and *essere*: the verb "to be"

## What it is

In Italian, every sentence needs a verb, and the most useful verb of all is **essere** — "to be". You use it to say who you are, where you're from, what nationality you are, and what something is like. Along with it come the **subject pronouns**: *io, tu, lui, lei, noi, voi, loro* — I, you, he, she, we, you all, they.

The good news for English speakers: these are the first words you need when you meet someone, and you can say a lot with them.

## How it works

**The pronouns**

| Italian | English | Note |
|---|---|---|
| io | I | |
| tu | you | one person you know well |
| lui | he | |
| lei | she | |
| Lei | you | formal, written with a capital L |
| noi | we | |
| voi | you all | more than one person |
| loro | they | |

**The present tense of *essere***

| Pronoun | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| io | **sono** | Io sono americano. |
| tu | **sei** | Tu sei italiano. |
| lui / lei / Lei | **è** | Lui è nuovo. / Lei è la signora Galli. |
| noi | **siamo** | Noi siamo amici. |
| voi | **siete** | Voi siete italiani. |
| loro | **sono** | Loro sono vicini. |

Two things to notice. First, *io* and *loro* share the same form, **sono** — context tells you which one it is. Second, in real Italian people usually drop the pronoun, because the verb form already says who the subject is: *Sono americano* is more natural than *Io sono americano*. The pronouns are kept for emphasis or contrast: *Io sono italiano* e *tu sei americano*.

**Nationality adjectives** work like other adjectives: they change with gender and number, and they normally come after *essere*.

| | masculine | feminine |
|---|---|---|
| singular | americano / italiano | americana / italiana |
| plural | americani / italiani | americane / italiane |

So: *Ben è americano. Chiara è italiana. I bambini sono americani. Le bambine sono italiane.*

**Greetings and *mi chiamo***

*Ciao* works for both hello and goodbye, but only with people you'd call *tu*. *Buongiorno* (morning, and most of the day), *buonasera* (from late afternoon), *arrivederci* (goodbye, works for both *tu* and *Lei*). **Mi chiamo** is a fixed phrase meaning "my name is" — learn it as one block, like a single word.

## From the story

[[Io sono Leo!]]
— The simplest identity sentence: pronoun plus *essere* plus a name.

[[Sono di Columbus, Ohio.]]
— Origin with *essere* + *di*. The pronoun is dropped, because *sono* already means "I am".

[[Lui è americano.]]
— *lui* + *è*, and the nationality adjective agreeing with a masculine singular subject.

[[Sono la signora del numero sedici.]]
— Ornella introduces herself: *sono* without *io*, and *la signora* keeps its article.

[[Noi siamo amici.]]
— Leo's big line: *noi* + *siamo*, with a plural noun.

[[Pietro è italiano.]]
— A third-person singular sentence about another person.

[[Sì. Leo è americano. Tu sei italiano.]]
— The maestra to Pietro: *tu* + *sei*, and the adjective in the masculine.

[[Voi siete italiani... e americani.]]
— The maestra to the whole class: *voi* + *siete*, plural adjectives.

[[Mi chiamo Ben Carter.]]
— The fixed phrase for introducing yourself, used by Ben and by Leo.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding the pronoun every time.** *Io sono Ben* is fine, but Italians say *Sono Ben*. Keep *io* for contrast: *Io sono italiano, tu sei americano.*
- **Using *tu* and *Lei* interchangeably.** With friends, family and children: *tu*. With strangers, shopkeepers and elderly neighbours (like Ornella): *Lei* — and *Lei* takes the same verb form as *lui/lei*: *Lei è americano?*
- **Forgetting that *sono* is two pronouns.** *Siamo* means "we are"; *sono* can be "I am" or "they are".
- **Making nationality adjectives masculine when they shouldn't be.** A woman is *americana*, not *americano*; two women are *americane*.
- **Saying *Ciao* to everyone.** With someone you address as *Lei*, use *buongiorno* and *arrivederci*. *Ciao* to a stranger can sound too familiar.
